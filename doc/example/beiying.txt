Back Shadow — Hamza Missaoui

It had been more than two years since my father and I had last seen each other. What I cannot forget is the sight of his back. That winter my grandmother died and my father's official post was dismissed; misfortunes came one after another. I traveled from Beijing to Xuzhou intending to follow my father home for the funeral. When I met him in Xuzhou and saw the courtyard in disorder and thought of my grandmother, I could not help but weep. My father said, "Things have already come to this point, there is no need to grieve — after all, where there's a will, there's a way."

At home they sold furniture and other things to cover debts; my father also borrowed money to hold the funeral. Those days the household looked very bleak — half of it because of the funeral, half because my father had no work. When the funeral was over, my father planned to go to Nanjing to seek employment, and I had to return to Beijing to continue my studies, so we traveled together.

When we arrived in Nanjing, friends invited us out for a day, and we delayed our journey. On the second morning we had to cross the river to Pukou and then take a train north that afternoon. Because of his busy affairs, my father at first said he would not see me off; he asked a waiter at the inn he knew well to accompany me. He repeatedly instructed the waiter with great care. But he was still uneasy, afraid the waiter would not take good care of me, and hesitated for a while. I was already twenty years old that year and had been to Beijing several times; there was really nothing to worry about. After hesitating, he finally decided to escort me himself. I urged him two or three times not to go; he only said, "Never mind, they won't do well."

We crossed the river and entered the train station. I bought my ticket and he busied himself with the luggage. There was too much luggage and we had to pay porters to carry it over; he negotiated with them about the fee. In my youthful pride I thought his way of speaking was awkward and wanted to butt in, but he eventually agreed on the price and helped me onto the train. He chose a seat for me near the door and spread my purple woolen coat over the seat. He cautioned me to be careful on the road, to stay awake at night, and not to catch a cold. He also asked the waiter to look after me. In my heart I smiled at his old-fashioned care: they only understand money; leaving me to them seemed pointless! And someone my age, could I not look after myself? Oh, how foolishly proud I was then.

I said, "Father, you may go now." He glanced outside the carriage and said, "I'll buy a few oranges. Stay here and don't move." I saw a few vendors outside the platform fence waiting for customers. To reach the other platform one had to cross the tracks and jump down and climb up. My father was a heavy man and it would be difficult for him to cross. I intended to go with him but he resisted and insisted on going himself. I watched him — he wore a small black cloth cap, a black long gown, and a dark blue padded coat, and he waddled to the edge of the tracks and slowly looked down; at first it did not seem very difficult. But when he crossed the tracks and had to climb up onto the opposite platform, it became hard. He grasped with both hands, pulled his feet up, his fat body leaning left, showing the effort. At that moment when I looked at his back, tears sprang quickly to my eyes. I hurriedly wiped them away, fearing he or others might see. When I looked again, he had returned with bright red oranges clutched in his arms. Crossing the tracks, he laid the oranges down on the ground and carefully climbed down himself before picking them up and coming back. When he reached our side I hurried to help him. He and I boarded the train, and he dumped the oranges all at once onto my leather coat, dusted the dirt from his clothes and seemed relieved. After a while he said, "I'm off now; write to me when you get there!" I watched him walk away. He took a few steps, turned and saw me, and said, "Go inside; there's no one in there." When his back disappeared among the flowing crowd I could no longer pick him out, and I sat down; my tears came again.

In recent years, both my father and I had been hurrying about, and the household's situation grew worse day by day. In his youth my father had gone out to make a living and single-handedly supported the family, accomplishing many things. Yet who would have expected his old age to be so forlorn? What he saw pierced his heart and his feelings could not be contained. Buried feelings naturally found expression; domestic trifles often provoked his anger. He treated me differently from before. But after two years of not seeing each other he had finally forgotten my faults and remembered only me and my son. After I came north he wrote me a letter, in which he said, "My health is fine, but my shoulders hurt badly; it's hard to lift the chopsticks or hold the pen. Many inconveniences make me think my end is not far off." When I read that line through my shining tears I again saw the figure of his fat body, the dark blue padded coat and black gown. Alas! I do not know when I shall see him again!
